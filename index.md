# Poetry and Madness
By Davis Diego Massey, winter 2025

Friedrich Hölderlin (1770–1843) was a poet and intellectual born in Lauffen am Neckar, now part of the country of Germany. Today, he is one of the most influential and well-respected German-language poets, but his reputation over the centuries has been fundamentally shaped by the long period of mental illness that he suffered for the second half of his life. For much of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, those who remembered him and wrote about him primarily interpreted his life and work through this condition of mental unwellness. They associated his late work with the reclusive and depressed life he led in a riverfront tower of Tübingen. 

While working as a tutor, he fell in love with his employer’s wife, Susette Gontard. They began a relationship that hovered over the line between romantic and platonic. Drawing from his admiration for Greek art, he addressed her in his poems as the artistic muse “Diotima.” In 1799, Gontard, who had inspired so much of his work, gently ended their relationship. In a final letter to her from that year, Hölderlin wrote “I have already had the thought we might be able to live from renunciation, as if it might perhaps give us strength if once and for all we said goodbye to hope.”[^note1]

Unhappy with a life working in odd jobs as a tutor or librarian, he began to embrace a life dedicated to his writing. He wrote to his mother, Johanna Christiana Gok, that working and searching for jobs had become exhausting to him, and that he only cared for his artistic pursuits: “if it weren’t for the inevitable disadvantage of my present way of life – that it won’t immediately satisfy my material needs – I should be content with it for ever.”[^note2] He grew increasingly depressed and erratic over the years. A few times, he suddenly decided to drop everything and take unplanned journeys across Europe. 

The public first became aware of the poet’s mental illness when he was put on trial for treason in 1805. His friend, Isaac von Sinclair, was accused of organizing a revolutionary secret society that planned to overthrow the Tübingen government, and Hölderlin was suspected of being involved. When he was brought before the court, however, he was showing signs of mental illness. A physician was brought in to evaluate whether he was fit to stand trial. The physician said: “His insanity has become a raving madness, and his speech - which seems part German, part Greek and part Latin - is quite unintelligible.”[^note3] The authorities released Hölderlin, believing that he did not understand what was going on and could not be held responsible for his actions. 

In 1806, Hölderlin was admitted to the care of University of Tübingen doctor Johann Heinrich Ferdinand von Autenrieth. Autenrieth officially diagnosed Hölderlin with madness, specifically “mania as an after-effect of scabies.”[^note4] Autenrieth believed that untreated skin rashes could lead to mania, a condition of abnormally intense emotions and mood swings. In the case of Hölderlin, he thought that his poetic inclinations made these symptoms worse, blaming his madness on “unhappy love, exhaustion, studies.”[^note5]

Autenrieth often used restraints on his patients limbs and faces, but due to the partial loss of records from his stay, we cannot be certain if he used these on Hölderlin. The remaining medical records show that Autenrieth used a regimen intended to treat physical causes for Hölderlin’s illness. The clinical staff gave belladonna leaves to sedate Hölderlin, mercury to purge his body through diarrhea, and aloe to clear out his gastric system. Autenrieth also used disciplinary measures to teach Hölderlin to work productively and be obedient. Of his therapeutic treatments for the mentally ill, the doctor said that he aimed to make a patient “as an irrational animal, obey the reason of another.”[^note6] While challenging their delusions with rational arguments, Autenrieth used moral treatments like chores in the clinic to try to make them learn how to be self-dependent and self-responsible. When Hölderlin cooperated, Autenrieth rewarded him with sightseeing walks into neighboring fields.

By 1807, Autenrieth discharged Hölderlin as incurable and gave him only 3 years to live. The doctor believed that, with the gastric and moral treatments having failed to cure him, there was no other option left for the poet. A local carpenter who admired Hölderlin, Ernst Zimmer, took him under his care and allowed him to live in a riverfront tower that he rented out to students. Zimmer reported to Hölderlin’s mother that he often suffered from irritability, insomnia, and long periods of apathetic unresponsiveness to the people around him. 

Students from the local university and admirers of his poetry often visited him, but he either ignored them or spoke to them in a cryptic way. He addressed his visitors with extremely formal language, addressing them as if they were royals, and became agitated when they did not return the favor. They reported that he often scrunched up his face as if under extreme mental duress, used French interspersed with German while speaking to them, and was easily frightened by any noises. He continued to write poetry on scraps of paper, take walks by the local Neckar River, and write letters to his family, who left him to the care of Zimmer. Hölderlin gave some insight into what he was thinking about in one undated letter to his mother, expressing thankfulness to God for allowing him to live to the present and writing: “I hope perhaps to continue my life without dangers and utter doubt.”[^note7]

During his later life, Hölderlin interpreted his own mental illness in his letters and private poems. In one poetic reflection on his sightseeing walks, he wrote that they soothed him "When dark are my mind and heart/Which paid from the beginning/In grief for thought and art."[^note8] However, the public did see most of these works while he was alive. Those visitors who did read them did not take them very seriously, assuming that they were the inferior output of a broken and sick man. As the modern psychiatric profession developed into a normal, everyday industry in late 19th century Germany, psychiatrists worked to engage with literature that the public was interested in. They sought to prove that they could make their own positive contribution to culture. Psychiatrists and the public began to look on the statements and works of the mentally ill with more seriousness. Psychiatrists began to retrospectively analyze and diagnose Hölderlin’s mental illness through the reports visitors wrote and even through the poetry he produced. The authors of psychiatric literature looked on him as a case study of an artist suffering madness, specifically from a depressive condition that they called catatonia. 

What modern historians can know about Hölderlin and his mental condition is shaped by how people treated him and remembered him as a madman. Even though he may not have met a 21st century definition of madness, since his symptoms were more typical of what we describe as depression today, the fact that people in the 19th and 20th century saw him as a classic case of a mad artist tells us about their attitudes towards mental health as much as about Hölderlin. This story centers around the ways that the public constructs and responds to mental illness, both during a mentally unwell person’s life and in the cultural remembrance of them.
